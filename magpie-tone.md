# Writing on Magpie.diy

Rules for every word that ships: UI labels, blurbs, help text, empty states, error
messages, emails, docs pages, changelog entries and landing page copy.

## The rule

**Say what the thing is, what the reader can do, or what happened. Nothing else.**

Text in this app helps the reader find something, decide what to do, or understand a
result. Keep it accurate, concrete and short. Do not add words to make a feature sound
important, a page sound substantial, or a writer sound personable.

## Accuracy comes first

- Do not invent what users commonly do, misunderstand, find frustrating, want, feel,
  skip, regret, or say. Make those claims only when the brief provides evidence.
- Do not invent why a feature was created, benefits that have not been established,
  promises about future results, or claims that something is easy, fast, safe or
  reliable without evidence.
- Do not write that something is *the most common mistake*, *a common frustration*,
  *this catches people out*, *most people start with*, *people skip this and regret
  it* or *what people will tell you*, or any rewording of them. None of it is known
  unless someone measured or said it.
- Describe the mechanism or observed fact instead of a story about user behavior.
  “A private entry is not shown on the public page” states what “People are often
  confused when their entry is missing from the public page” was reaching for, and it
  is true.
- A draft that invents user psychology to give a section a reason to exist has a
  section with no reason to exist. Delete the section instead of justifying it.
- When information is missing, ask for it or use a clear placeholder. Do not hide the
  gap with broad or polished language.
- Describe the exact effect. Do not use *deleted*, *removed*, *cleared*, *disabled*,
  *reset* or *replaced* unless that is what the software does. A setting that remains
  stored but does not apply should be described that way.
- Use the product's exact terminology. Do not substitute a synonym just to avoid
  repetition. Use the label shown in the interface when that is the clearest name.

AI tools may draft boilerplate or placeholder copy. A person must review copy that is
intended to remain in the product. Drafts should follow this guide before review; in
particular, do not let a draft fill gaps with invented user behavior, motives, benefits
or feelings.

## Tone

- **No marketing** outside the acquisition landing page exception below. Do not brag,
  compare Magpie with other products, pressure readers, create urgency, or assume they
  have decided to buy or act. Do not call something unusually thoughtful, elegant or
  carefully designed. Do not write *Don't miss out*, *Get started today*, *What are you
  waiting for?* or *You'll love it*.
- **No performed personality.** Do not add jokes, knowing asides, fake admissions,
  verbal winks, rhetorical questions, slogans, or casual phrasing for its own sake.
  Examples: *Let's be honest*, *Here's the thing*, *Pretty neat, right?*, *Yes,
  really*, *It's kind of our thing*. A sentence must not call attention to how casual,
  candid or clever its writer is.
- **No manufactured empathy or reassurance.** Do not claim to know what the reader feels
  or wants, as in *We know how frustrating this can be*, *We get it* or *You deserve
  better*. When something goes wrong, explain what happened, what it affected and what
  the reader can do.
- **No unnecessary positivity or negativity.** Avoid praise, celebration, nostalgia,
  decline narratives, disappointment and reassurance unless the judgment itself is
  relevant and supported.
- **No hedging or apology.** Avoid words such as *a bit*, *kind of*, *unfortunately*,
  *we're afraid* and *please note*. Apologize only when something was actually lost;
  name what was lost and what happens next.
- **No AI boilerplate.** Avoid polished but empty phrases such as *seamless*,
  *effortless*, *elevate*, *empower*, *unlock*, *robust*, *intuitive*, *tailored to your
  needs*, *designed with you in mind*, *take your experience to the next level*,
  *whether you are new or experienced*, *at the end of the day* and *more than just*.
  Also avoid these common AI-writing tells: *delve*, *tapestry*, *realm*, *pivotal*,
  *intricate*, *meticulous*, *underscore*, *showcase*, *foster*, *navigate*,
  *landscape*, *testament*, *serves as*, *stands as*, *a testament to*, *in today's …
  landscape*, *it's not X, it's Y*, *not just X, but Y* and *whether you're X or Y*.
  The lists are warnings, not a substitute for checking whether the phrase says
  something precise. Do not replace a literal statement with a polished synonym,
  metaphor, contrast or rhetorical construction.
- Do not announce the principles in the copy. Show clarity by writing clearly; do not
  say that Magpie respects the reader's time or keeps things simple.

## Essay voice and mood

State the fact. Do not turn it into a reflection on how things used to be, what was
lost, what something means or why it matters. A short, suggestive sentence asks the
reader to supply the significance and adds no information.

**Avoid:** Somewhere along the way, shared calendars became a mess.

**Prefer:** A team calendar shows every member's events in one view.

These constructions are warnings that a sentence may be creating mood or significance
instead of stating a fact: *used to be full of*, *there was a time when*, *somewhere
along the way*, *what has not changed is*, *the interesting thing is*, *the important
part is*, *there is something about*, *it is easy to forget that*, *in its own way*,
*that is the point*, *and maybe that is enough*. A sentence built on *these*, *this*,
*that* or *it* should name the thing when the vagueness is there for effect instead of
because the reference is already clear.

Test: could the sentence open or close a personal essay? If so, replace it with the
concrete fact it comments on.

Do not add a mood the information does not require: nostalgia for a supposedly better
past, claims that something good was lost, claims that technology or culture has become
worse, complaints about other products, forced optimism about a feature, reassurance
when nothing worrying happened, or celebration of an ordinary fact.

**Avoid:** Fortunately, you still have complete control over who can see a note.

**Prefer:** You choose who can see a note.

A judgment is allowed when it is relevant and supportable, such as a specific
disadvantage that is the subject of the page. Test: if the facts stayed the same, could
the sentence make the reader feel better or worse, and is that effect part of the
page's purpose? If not, remove it. Let the reader decide whether the fact is good or
bad.

## Literal wording

Describe what an interface element does or contains. Prefer verbs such as *has*,
*shows*, *contains*, *uses*, *adds*, *removes*, *opens*, *closes*, *sends*, *blocks*,
*allows* and *requires* when they state the behavior accurately.

- Do not replace a literal explanation with a metaphor, idiom, personification or
  colorful turn of phrase. A toolbar does not get out of the way; it appears after an
  item is selected. Familiar and casual phrases count: *left to fend for itself*, *gets
  out of the way*, *fights for space*, *falls apart*, *comes to the rescue*, *does the
  heavy lifting* and *gets in your way* each hide a more precise statement.
- Do not give software or records a body or mind. A page, setting, file, entry or date
  cannot *wear*, *carry*, *sit*, *live*, *rest*, *belong*, *govern*, *invite*,
  *deserve*, *breathe*, *want*, *know* or *care*. Use the exact relationship: a draft is
  stored in an account; a setting controls access; an entry is positioned at a date.
  These verbs look plain, so they are easy to miss, and they carry less information:
  “sits at August 15” does not say whether that is the entry's position, its label or
  where it is stored. Replace the verb when its subject could not do it without a body
  or mind, or when a plain verb such as *is*, *appears*, *goes*, *is stored*, *is
  positioned* or *controls* fits the same space.
- Avoid adverbs that add tone instead of information, including *quietly*, *simply*,
  *easily*, *smoothly*, *seamlessly*, *neatly* and *effortlessly*. Keep an adverb when
  it communicates a necessary fact such as timing or frequency. Do not use *quiet* or
  *quietly* in copy.
- Do not use *here* or *there* to mean Magpie, the site, its users or a feature. Name
  the page, team, account or feature, or omit the location.
- Prefer ordinary words and straightforward sentences. Do not make simple facts sound
  significant, polished or profound.
- Never write *workspace* in anything a user can see, including UI, Help, errors,
  email, changelog entries and admin pages. The shared unit is a **team**; the
  other scope is **Personal**. Write “Personal or the selected team”, not “the
  selected workspace”. The Dashboard chat is the **assistant**. Class names,
  routes, config keys and log events may keep *workspace* internally.

| Avoid | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| The mobile layout was left to fend for itself. | The layout was designed for desktop and was not adapted for smaller screens. |
| The controls fight for space on a phone. | The controls take up too much space on a phone. |
| The table falls apart on smaller screens. | The table requires horizontal scrolling on smaller screens. |
| Six columns were never going to survive on a phone. | Six columns do not fit on a phone. |
| Your drafts live in your account until you publish them. | Your drafts are stored in your account until you publish them. |
| This setting governs who can read an entry. | This setting controls who can read an entry. |
| Find the date where the entry belongs. | Choose the date to place the entry on. |

## Help and reference copy

Help and reference copy is colder than other public copy. Explain the feature, state
its limits and stop. Do not add warmth, encouragement, jokes, speculation about the
reader's situation or motives, aphorisms, or a closing line that reaches for
significance. Prefer a flat, exact sentence over an interesting sentence when both
are clear. Underwriting is easy to fix; invented detail can look authoritative.

Do not add a “what this is not” section unless a real, specific confusion needs an
answer. Do not add an opening paragraph just to introduce a page, or an ending just to
make it feel complete.

## Tense and point of view

Write for the moment in which the reader sees the copy. A review screen, confirmation,
setting description or Help article often describes what will happen after the reader
acts; use the future tense for that result. Use the imperative for instructions and
the past tense for completed actions.

| Moment | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before an action: its result | Future | “Your changes will apply to new invoices.” |
| The instruction | Imperative | “Choose a file.” |
| After an action: its result | Past | “The invoice was sent.” |

Keep the present tense for what is already true: what something is (“A draft is an
entry that has not been posted.”), what is true now (“You have three imports
waiting.”), and what a page shows or contains (“The page lists every import you have
not finished.”). Do not describe a future action as a timeless fact.

| Avoid | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| An admin gets an alert and comes back to you. | An admin will get an alert and come back to you. |
| Your entries keep the dates they were written on. | Your entries will keep the dates they were written on. |
| The import runs in the background and emails you when it is done. | The import will run in the background and will email you when it is finished. |

The timeless present is wrong for a result for three reasons. It documents the
mechanism instead of addressing the reader who is deciding whether to act. It states a
promise as though it were a law of nature, so nobody is responsible for it; the future
names an undertaking that can be relied on. It is also the usual voice of generated
copy, which reads like a specification sheet even when the words are plain.

Use an active sentence when the actor matters. Do not hide an action inside a vague
statement that something is happening or being done. Passive voice is fine when the
actor is unknown, irrelevant or the passive is clearer. Avoid stacking passive clauses
until every actor disappears. Delete passive observations that do not help the reader;
do not rewrite unnecessary commentary just to make it active.

A passive sentence that only reports a general condition is usually filler: “Personal
websites are still being made.” “New pages are being created every day.” Ask who does
the thing and name them when it matters (“People still make personal websites.” “You
upload files through the editor.”). If the reader does not need the observation,
delete it; making it active keeps the filler. Look closely at *still*, *continue to*,
*remain*, *can be*, *are being*, *is being* and *have been* when they describe a broad
trend or condition.

A chain of passives hides every actor: “A file nothing can read yet is kept and its
structure recorded, an admin is told, and somebody will get in touch.” Name who acts:
“An admin will get an alert, work out how to read the format, and come back to you.”
To check a sentence, read its first seven or eight words and look for a character
followed by an action.

## Errors and failure messages

An error message names what failed and what the reader can do next. Report the cause,
not only the fact that an action failed.

- If the cause is known, state it and give the next step when one exists: “That email
  address is already registered. Sign in instead.”
- If the cause is unknown, state what was affected and what remains possible: “The entry
  was not saved. Its text is still in the editor, so you can try again.”
- Do not expose internal exception details to readers.
- Do not use *Oops*, *Uh oh*, *Whoops* or exclamation marks.

## Changelog copy

Changelog entries record changes; they do not tell a story about them. Start with the
change, use a literal title and stop when the changed behavior is clear.

- Prefer direct release-note wording such as *Added*, *Changed*, *Fixed* and *Removed*.
- Use *we* only when the sentence genuinely refers to more than one person.
- State old and new behavior only when both help explain the change.
- Do not add scene-setting, a justification, product framing or commentary just to make
  the entry more interesting. Do not turn the title into a miniature essay.
- Use the product's exact terms and describe the exact effect. Do not vary terminology
  for style, especially in summaries of the entry.
- Do not personify interface elements or add adverbs to make a technical change sound
  subtle or elegant.
- Put normal behavior first. Keep an exception or side effect after it, and include it
  only if it changes what the reader should expect or do. Implementation importance
  does not make a detail important to the reader.
- Do not open with a story about the problem. Avoid “A library of thousands of files
  needs something better than file names.” Prefer “Added tags for images.”
- Repeat the exact term. A summary may reuse the nouns and verbs of the body. Avoid
  “Blocking one account also quietly extends to its related accounts.” Prefer “Blocking
  one account also blocks its linked accounts.”
- Choose the verb that matches the effect. If a setting is kept but stops applying,
  avoid “Unlocking a diary removes shared logins.” Prefer “Shared logins apply only
  while a diary is locked.”
- Use a literal title. Avoid “A page for what has changed.” Prefer “New changelog.”
  Avoid “Some public pages wore a cut-down top bar. All of them now carry the same
  destinations.” Prefer “Fixed missing navigation items on some public pages.”
- When one accurate sentence explains the change, use one sentence.

## Structure and emphasis

Start with useful information. Use literal headings that name the content below them.
Avoid throat-clearing, abstract headings that could fit hundreds of unrelated pages,
unnecessary conclusions, repeated points, forced parallel sentence structures, and
secondary implementation details promoted into the opening just because they were
recently changed.

A heading names the information in its section. Do not invent an abstract phrase to
group concrete facts. *What a site is made of*, *How it all fits together*, *What
happens behind the scenes*, *The pieces that make it work* and *Everything in one
place* name no subject. Use the concrete noun or action: *HTML and CSS files*,
*Starter files*, *Uploading files*. If a heading would fit unchanged above hundreds of
unrelated pages, it does not name the section. Split the section or use a narrower
heading instead of a broader abstraction.

A page does not need to feel substantial. A short page that contains the useful
information is complete. Leave out greetings and sign-offs in interface and Help copy,
and avoid rhetorical questions and exclamation marks.

Do not add stock page sections that say little: numbered “how it works” cards, feature
grids with an icon and a line that repeats the heading, adjective triplets, unsupported
statistics, testimonial or logo strips, “Why choose us,” “Who it's for,” or “Ready to
start?” panels. If a process needs explanation, describe only the necessary steps in
the order the reader encounters them. Every section must contain information specific
to its page.

## Sentence and paragraph structure

- Put actions in verbs and name the actor when it matters. Keep the subject and
  verb close enough that a reader does not have to reconstruct the sentence.
- Begin with context the reader recognizes, then introduce new information.
  Put the detail needing emphasis near the sentence's end when that order is clear.
- Give each paragraph one identifiable point. Order explanations by the reader's
  task or the relationship between ideas, rather than by when you discovered them.
- Use parallel structure inside a list of comparable actions or facts. Do not shape two
  separate sentences into matching halves to make a contrast sound balanced; state each
  fact in its clearest form. Avoid “A diary is in the order you wrote it. A site is in
  the order you decide.” Prefer “Unlike diary entries, site pages do not have to appear
  by date. You can organize them by subject.” Long sentences are fine when their
  relationships remain clear; short sentences are not a goal by themselves.
- Remove repeated meaning, implied information, empty modifiers, and unnecessary
  introductions. Preserve complexity and qualification when accuracy requires them.

## Do

- Lead with the noun or verb: “Contact list.” “Create and send invoices.”
- Use plain words: “Use,” not “utilise”; “Send,” not “deliver.”
- Say who or what is involved when that is the point: “Text chat with your team” is
  more useful than “Text chat channels.”
- Keep labels to one line. If a label needs two sentences, improve the surrounding UI.
- Use sentence case for everything except proper nouns and page titles.
- For empty states about team-owned collections, name the team scope. Use
  `scope_label()` in templates.

## The exception: acquisition landing pages

`resources/views/pages/data-removal-offer.twig` is allowed to sell. It is the
page someone lands on from a mailing about Data Removal, and it
has to make an argument rather than label a control: what the customer wants to change,
how the service produces that result, why the claim is credible, what it costs, and the
scope that affects the purchase.

Public feature pages rendered by `resources/views/pages/feature-marketing.twig`
are acquisition pages too, with a narrower exception. Their copy lives in
`resources/marketing/pages/` and `resources/marketing/pending-pages.json`.
They persuade through specific capabilities, useful examples and accurate product
detail. A hero may have landing-page framing, but it must identify the feature and
explain what Magpie's implementation does. Generic slogans, unsupported benefits,
urgency, hype and conventional SaaS filler remain prohibited.

Select information that helps someone evaluate a workflow, team permissions, outside
access, sharing, connections to other features or a material limit. Verify product
claims against the current implementation. Do not mechanically turn each fact into
a benefit, pad simple pages, repeat capabilities across sections or add FAQs just
to complete a page pattern. Setup minutiae belong in Help unless they affect the
decision to use the feature.

A feature-page FAQ explains what Magpie does; it never answers "No." Do not
add a question whose answer is that Magpie lacks something, and do not point
readers to other services. If no question earns a place with a substantive,
affirmative answer, the page has no FAQ. Screenshots, relevant Help links and signup controls
remain useful acquisition structure.

The following outcome-led guidance applies to the Data Removal offer.

**Customer-centric is a selection rule, not a sentence template.** Lead with the result
the customer is buying, then explain only the product mechanics needed to understand or
believe it. Do not mechanically append a benefit to every feature: a process step,
price or form requirement is often clearest as a literal fact.

Before adding a fact, ask whether it helps a prospective customer decide that the
service is relevant, credible and worth the price. A fact does not earn space merely
because it is true or implemented. In particular:

- **Outcomes lead.** State the useful change before the internal mechanism that
  produces it.
- **Mechanics explain the offer.** Include what the service and customer actually do
  when that affects the buying decision. Setup instructions belong in onboarding and
  Help unless they resolve a real pre-purchase concern. Do not elevate a replaceable
  mechanism, such as individual opt-out forms, over the result the customer is paying
  Magpie to produce.
- **Evidence reduces uncertainty.** Verifiable coverage, real results, independent
  assessment and an actual refund policy can establish credibility. Internal queues,
  review delays, storage architecture and other implementation details do not become
  proof merely because they are specific.
- **FAQ space is earned.** Answer questions a prospective customer reasonably needs
  before buying. Do not inventory every feature, policy or edge case.
- **Scope stays proportionate.** State material limits without making the main
  argument revolve around them. Detailed policies belong in the FAQ, Help, legal pages
  or security documentation according to what the reader needs.

Page sections have to earn their space by the same test. Do not add a conventional
landing-page section such as “How it works,” “Why us,” or a three-step process merely
because other sites have one. Keep it only when it communicates a customer outcome,
credible evidence, a material buying choice, or the answer to a real objection more
clearly than the surrounding copy can. Put necessary setup and process details in the
FAQ when they matter before purchase but are not part of the main argument.

The exception is written down here rather than left as a silent inconsistency, and it
is deliberately narrow:

- **It covers the Data Removal offer and public feature pages only.** Nav labels,
  app blurbs, buttons, empty states,
  errors, Help and the `/billing` page stay dry. A sentence written for the offer page
  does not get reused in the app.
- **It does not license inaccuracy.** Every claim about coverage on that page is
  conditional on a real number (see `DataRemovalCoverage`), material scope is stated
  where it affects the decision, and the renewal price sits next to the offer price
  rather than at checkout. Selling is allowed; overstating is not.
- **It does not license the usual tricks.** No countdown timers, no invented scarcity,
  no fake testimonials or counts. An offer expires because a real date passed, not
  because a page said so.

If another kind of acquisition page is built, add its scope to this list rather than
widening the rule.

## When rules conflict

Apply them in this order:

1. Be accurate.
2. Include the information the reader needs.
3. Be clear.
4. Be direct.
5. Follow the tone rules above.

Do not give up accuracy or clarity to make copy warmer, smoother, more persuasive or
more entertaining.

## Final editing pass

Before treating a draft as complete, check the following:

- Does the opening contain useful information? Could the first paragraph be deleted?
- Does the ending add information, or does it only sound like an ending?
- Is future tense used for results the reader has not caused yet, imperative for
  instructions and past tense for completed actions?
- Does every claim about users, benefits, quality or results have evidence?
- Is any point repeated, hedged, apologized for, exaggerated or stated more than once?
- Is there a joke, aside, rhetorical question, verbal wink, slogan or performed
  personality?
- Does any sentence add nostalgia, optimism, pessimism, reassurance or disappointment
  that the information does not require?
- Could a metaphor, idiom, figurative verb or personification be replaced with the
  literal behavior?
- Does an adverb add necessary information, or only tone?
- Does *here* or *there* treat Magpie as a place?
- Does an error name the cause and next step, or explain what was affected when the
  cause is unknown?
- Does a Help heading name its section? Does each section contain information specific
  to this page?
- Is an exception taking more emphasis than the normal behavior or the reader needs?
- Does changelog copy state the change with the product's exact terminology and effect?
- Does any sentence say what users commonly do, find confusing or regret without
  evidence? Does a section exist only because of invented user psychology?
- Could any sentence open or close a personal essay? Does it add a mood the information
  does not require?
- Does a verb need a body or mind that its subject lacks?
- Does a passive sentence only report a general condition, or hide who acts?
- Could a heading appear unchanged above hundreds of unrelated pages?
- Are two sentences matched in form at the cost of saying either one naturally?
- Could any sentence be shorter without losing accuracy or clarity?

If a sentence exists mainly to sound friendly, polished, clever, reassuring or human,
remove it and check whether any useful information was lost.
